From rain to runoff

What CaMa-Flood starts from.

A land surface model splits the rainone grid cell, seen from the side
Runoff on a regular grida rate per cell, mm per day

Rain meets the land

A land surface model (such as MATSIRO, VIC, JULES, or HTESSEL in ERA5-Land) takes the precipitation on each grid cell and splits it. Some evaporates or is transpired by plants, some soaks into the soil, and the rest leaves the land: as surface runoff when the ground cannot take more, and as subsurface runoff draining out of the soil into the streams.

Runoff on a grid

The column we followed is one cell of a regular grid that covers all land on Earth. For every cell the model reports a runoff rate, typically daily or hourly; the runs on this page use the daily 0.25° runoff of the eartH2Observe WRR2 reanalysis from ECMWF's HTESSEL. This runoff is CaMa-Flood's input: from here on, the model moves the water the land has released.

Unit catchments

Seen from closer, the cell is covered by parts of several unit catchments. CaMa-Flood traces them on high-resolution hydrography (MERIT Hydro; Yamazaki et al., 2019). Each catchment holds one river reach with its floodplain and drains into exactly one downstream catchment.

Runoff mapping

Grid cells and catchments do not line up. The area of every fine pixel is added to a weight matrix W[catchment, cell]; W is almost all zeros, so only its non-zero overlaps are stored. The orange catchment, for example, collects the runoff of the few cells it overlaps, each weighted by its share of pixels. Multiplying by W does this for every catchment at once.

Storage and flow

Water storage is the one quantity the model carries forward: each unit catchment gains runoff and the discharge of its upstream catchments, and loses its own discharge downstream. The sub-grid topography turns storage into a water level. Below the bank the river channel fills; above it, the flood table, which lists how high the water stands when 10%, 20%, … of the catchment is flooded, gives the flood depth and flooded area. The discharge to the next catchment follows the local inertial equation (Yamazaki et al., 2013), driven by the drop of the water surface between the two outlets. The two levels steer each other: discharge lowers the upstream level and raises the downstream one, which flattens the drop and slows the flow; when the downstream catchment fills from elsewhere, the drop shrinks and water backs up, even into reverse flow.

Yamazaki et al. (2011, 2013) →

The CFL condition

A disturbance on the water surface travels at about √(gh), faster in deeper water. For the equations to stay stable, a wave may cross only part of a catchment in one time step: Δt ≤ αL/√(gh), the Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy (CFL) condition. As a flood deepens the rivers, the time step shrinks, and it grows again as the water falls.

On the GPU

The GPU never waits for the disk

Two loader processes read the coming days of runoff while the GPU simulates the current one, and the data reach the GPU in the background. There a single sparse matrix product maps all runoff cells to catchments. Finished results go to two writer processes that compress them and append them to NetCDF, handed over through shared memory by HydroForge, so reading, computing and writing overlap.

Configuration →

Every catchment at once

Catchments are numbered from upstream to downstream and stored as plain arrays. One GPU thread per catchment computes its discharge, and the fluxes are added into the downstream storages with atomic adds, so confluences need no ordering.

Statistics on the device

Statistics are computed on the GPU while the model runs. Here two are saved from the same discharge: the daily mean, which leaves the GPU when each day ends, and the monthly maximum of those daily means, which stays on the GPU until the month closes. Both are declared once and compiled by HydroForge into the same update on every backend; background writers append them to NetCDF while the GPU keeps computing.

Statistics and output →

Whole basins per GPU

In the model no water moves from one river basin to another, so basins are the unit of work. torchrun starts one process per GPU, and HydroForge hands each of them whole basins, so a GPU reads only the runoff cells its catchments need.

  • Triton, CUDA and MetalTriton by default, CUDA C++ compiled at start-up, and Metal on Apple GPUs, where double precision is emulated with two 32-bit numbers.
  • Mixed precision32-bit arithmetic with water volumes kept in double precision, so small changes survive rounding.
  • Step replayThe kernels of a model step are recorded once as a CUDA graph and replayed every step.
  • Adaptive time stepThe time step follows the CFL condition of the deepest river, day by day.
  • Optional physicsBifurcation channels, levees and dam reservoirs, each a module you switch on.
  • CheckpointsThe full model state goes to a file and a run restarts from it.
